# Archetype Long-form Reference

Use this reference for 5–10 minute stories about a recognizable type of person: “顶级嘉豪的一生”, a workplace striver, a social climber, a chronic bluffer, a control freak, or a familiar-person archetype. Create an original fictional composite; do not target, identify, or encourage harassment of a real person.

## The correct unit: a set piece, not an example

An archetype episode is not one plot with extra commentary. It is a chain of 4–6 memorable set pieces. Each set piece gives the viewer a complete small emotional meal and leaves a problem that causes the next scene.

`trigger → ritualized action → private self-narration → external countershot → physical/social residue → next trigger`

Example of the abstract pattern (do not copy scenes or wording from a benchmark):

| Beat | Purpose |
|---|---|
| A minor social cue | Opens the protagonist’s need to be seen. |
| An elaborate display | Shows a distinctive trait through body, prop, and language. |
| The inner interpretation | Lets the viewer understand why the action feels heroic to them. |
| A social countershot | Reveals the room’s actual reading without a narrator calling the person “ridiculous”. |
| A residue | Leaves a message, smell, recording, debt, object, silence, or damaged relationship. |
| A bridge | The residue becomes the trigger for the next attempted recovery. |

## Persona-feature ledger

Choose five to seven distinct features. For each, write a different setting, object, body behavior, phrase, and social reaction. Use the ledger to prevent generic biography.

| Feature dimension | Questions |
|---|---|
| Status ritual | What tiny ceremony makes them feel important? |
| Language | What evasive, oversized, or defensive phrase do they repeat? |
| Body | What pause, posture, touch, breath, or gait gives the game away? |
| Prop | What object helps them borrow status or hide insecurity? |
| Cognitive distortion | What do they sincerely think other people’s reaction means? |
| Group response | Who watches, who avoids, who records, who enables, who is harmed? |
| Cost | What gets more permanent after this scene: a record, a rumour, a rupture, a debt, or a public role? |

Give the character a private need, but do not use it as an excuse. The viewer should understand the choice and still feel its cost.

## Dramatic-irony engine

For social-comedy, cringe, and self-deception stories, keep two versions of every key moment alive:

| Protagonist’s reading | Viewer’s reading |
|---|---|
| “They are silent because I have presence.” | A close-up shows discomfort, suppressed laughter, avoidance, or a recording phone. |
| “They asked me to perform because they admire me.” | The crowd’s exchange or earlier setup shows a joke at their expense. |
| “I am leaving on my own terms.” | The room has simply stopped engaging; the exit leaves an awkward residue. |

Do not write “he is embarrassing.” Let a countershot, changed distance, object, or sound cue make the audience feel it. Reveal the social truth early, then let the protagonist choose a larger display to repair their imagined status.

## Escalation map for eight minutes

Use four or five acts of roughly 70–110 seconds; each contains 3–5 micro-beats and one localized payoff.

1. **The self-authored entrance.** Establish the character’s ritual, self-image, and the group’s first countershot.
2. **The competence display.** They borrow the language or objects of a higher-status world; reality intrudes through a concrete detail.
3. **The recorded residue.** A group, chat, receipt, call, or witness makes the consequence portable and irreversible.
4. **The escalation in a bigger arena.** The character mistakes irony, politeness, or provocation for admiration and performs harder.
5. **The pattern completes.** Show the final social cost or recognition. A redemption is optional; never attach a generic moral in place of an ending image.

Move from private body detail → classmates/colleagues → digital trace → crowd/public space → lasting residue. The arena should change even when the personality does not.

## Transition ledger

Write one causal bridge per set piece before drafting narration:

| End of current set piece | Next trigger |
|---|---|
| Ignored display | The character tries a louder borrowed identity. |
| A recording/rumour | They overcorrect to regain control of the narrative. |
| Physical discomfort or broken prop | It creates a visible risk in the next public setting. |
| Ironical invitation | They mistake it for a genuine invitation and commit publicly. |

If the next segment is only “another thing this type of person does”, reject it. It must answer the prior scene’s unresolved social need.

## Drafting and audit

- Use second person only if it increases discomfort/immersion; do not convert every “he” into “you” mechanically.
- Alternate close sensory details with wide social countershots. A long episode needs both the character’s internal delusion and the room’s observable reaction.
- Give every 15–30 seconds a micro-turn: new action, new witness, exposed mismatch, relocated arena, larger commitment, or changed meaning.
- Let the central person cause every escalation. Avoid importing a late antagonist or financial/legal mechanism merely to manufacture a climax.
- Do not copy a benchmark’s sequence, phrases, visual design, or specific scenes. Abstract its retention structure only.

Before delivery, ask: Can I name five distinct behaviors? Does each become costlier in a new social arena? Does the audience know the truth before the character? Does every scene cause the next? If any answer is no, rebuild the set-piece map.
